Stations are sorted by mile marker along the route. The destination is a virtual station with price `0`. Fuel is tracked in miles of range: a 500-mile tank at 10 MPG holds 50 gallons.

The service minimises **fuel cost + `stop_penalty` × number of stops** (default **$15 per stop**, about 30 minutes of driver time). With the penalty at `0`, a simple greedy is optimal, and it is the easiest way to see what a good plan looks like. With a penalty, the service runs a dynamic program instead.

## Free stops: the greedy intuition

Standing at a station with price `p` and some fuel left, look at every station within the next 500 miles:

- **A cheaper station is in range.** Buy just enough to reach the *first* one, then drive there. If the fuel in the tank already reaches it, buy nothing.
- **Nothing cheaper is in range.** Fill the tank here, then drive to the *cheapest* station in range.
- **No station is in range** and the destination is further than 500 miles: return `422 unreachable`, naming the gap.

### Why greedy is optimal when stops are free

Every mile of the trip needs fuel bought somewhere before it. The cheapest station that can still reach a given mile is the best place to buy the fuel for it. The rule does exactly that:

- If something cheaper lies ahead within range, fuel bought here beyond what it takes to get there could have been bought cheaper later. So it buys the minimum.
- If nothing cheaper lies within range, every mile of the next 500 will be fuelled at this price or worse. So it buys as much as the tank holds, and moves to the best place to top up next.

An exchange argument makes it formal: take any optimal plan that disagrees with greedy at the first station where they differ, move gallons from the more expensive station to the cheaper one, and the cost never increases. This is the classic gas-station problem (Khuller, Malekian, Mestre, 2007) on a line, where the greedy solution is exact.

### Worked example

A 1,100-mile trip with six stations. Step through it, or shuffle the prices and watch the plan change.

| At | Fuel on arrival | In range (next 500 mi) | Decision | Bought |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | 500 mi | A, B, C | Can't buy here. Drive to A. | — |
| A · $3.40 | 380 mi | B $3.05 is cheaper | B is reachable already. Buy nothing. | — |
| B · $3.05 | 200 mi | D $2.95 is cheaper | Need 310 mi, have 200. Buy 11 gal. | $33.55 |
| D · $2.95 | 0 mi | Finish (price 0) | Need 490 mi. Buy 49 gal. | $144.55 |

Total: **60 gallons for $178.10**. Nothing is bought at A, and the truck drives straight past C: B bought exactly enough to reach the cheaper D.

## Priced stops: a dynamic program

On real corridors the greedy chases cents. NYC → Los Angeles has 447 corridor stations, and with free stops the cheapest plan makes 16 stops, many of them small top-ups to reach a station two cents cheaper. A stop costs time, so the service gives each one a price, and greedy is no longer optimal: it never weighs "one more stop" against "a few cents per gallon".

The service solves it exactly with a dynamic program over **(station, fuel on arrival)**:

- `dp[f]` is the cheapest way to arrive at the current station with `f` miles of fuel, for `f` from 0 to 500. It starts with a full tank at mile 0.
- **At a station** with price `p`, either drive on (no cost), or stop: pay the penalty and fill from some `f0 ≤ f` up to `f`. That is `min over f0 ≤ f of dp[f0] + p·(f − f0) + penalty`. Rewritten as `p·f + penalty + min(dp[f0] − p·f0)`, the inner minimum is a running minimum, so one pass over the 501 levels handles the station.
- **Driving** `d` miles to the next station shifts the array: `f` becomes `f − d`; negative fuel is dropped.
- At the destination any `f ≥ 0` is fine. The cheapest one, followed back through stored back-pointers, gives the stops and how much each one bought.

Complexity is **O(n · F)**: n corridor stations, F = 501 fuel levels. NYC → LA (447 stations) runs in about 22 ms in pure Python.

Fuel is counted in **whole miles** (0.1 gal). Station positions round up to the next mile, and the tank cap rounds down, so every plan the DP returns can really be driven; the cost of that rounding is at most a fraction of a gallon per stop. The test suite checks the DP against a brute force that tries every purchase at every station, with and without a penalty.

### Choosing the penalty

| `stop_penalty` | NYC → LA stops | Fuel cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 16 | $698.92 |
| 5–10 | 9 | $700.05 |
| **15 (default)** | **7** | **$702.79** |
| 20–30 | 7 | $702.79 |
| 40 | 6 | $705.93 |

$15 removes nine stops for $3.87 more fuel. Dallas → Chicago drops from 4 stops to 2 (+$0.14), Miami → Seattle from 20 to 8 (+$3.46). The penalty only steers the choice: `total_fuel_cost` is still fuel money only. Pass `stop_penalty=0` for the cheapest-fuel plan.

## Implementation notes

- `Station.price` is a `DecimalField`. The DP compares candidate plans in floats for speed; the chosen purchases are then priced in `Decimal` (gallons = whole miles ÷ 10), so the reported costs are exact to the cent.
- Positions are city centroids, so a mile marker can be off by a few miles. See [Limits](/docs/limits).
